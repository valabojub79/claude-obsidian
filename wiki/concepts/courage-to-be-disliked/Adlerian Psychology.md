# Adlerian Psychology

The school of thought founded by [[Alfred Adler]], formally called Individual Psychology. It holds that humans are indivisible wholes, driven by social interest and future goals rather than fragmented by instincts or trapped by the past. Among the three founding traditions of depth psychology (Freud, Jung, Adler), it is the most optimistic and the least deterministic.

## Core Principles

### 1. Teleology, Not Etiology

Behavior is understood by its purpose (where a person is heading) not by its causes (what happened before). You are not determined by your past. You use your past — selectively — to justify the goals you currently hold. See [[Teleology vs Etiology]].

### 2. All Problems Are Interpersonal

Adler: "All problems are interpersonal relationship problems." Anxiety, self-loathing, social withdrawal, anger — all have their roots in how a person navigates relationships with others. This reframes therapy from intrapsychic excavation to interpersonal skill.

### 3. Lifestyle

Each person develops a consistent "lifestyle" — a set of beliefs about self, world, and what one must do to belong. This is chosen, not imposed. It typically crystallizes in childhood but can be changed at any moment by deciding to change it.

### 4. Inferiority Feelings as Universal Motivation

All humans start life in a state of inferiority (small, helpless, dependent). The drive to overcome this is the engine of human development. Healthy response: channel inferiority into growth. Pathological response: freeze into an inferiority complex (using inferiority as excuse) or superiority complex (overcompensating through arrogance). See [[Inferiority Feelings]].

### 5. Social Interest (Gemeinschaftsgefuhl)

The goal of psychological development is community feeling — a sense of belonging to and contributing to something beyond the self. Mental illness, in Adler's view, is fundamentally a loss of social interest. See [[Community Feeling]].

### 6. Separation of Tasks

Each situation belongs to one person's "task" to resolve. Entering another person's task (through unsolicited advice, control, or worry) creates conflict. Freedom requires both doing your own tasks and leaving others to do theirs. See [[Separation of Tasks]].

### 7. Horizontal Relationships

Vertical relationships (better/worse, praise/rebuke, competition) produce psychological stress. Horizontal relationships (equal, cooperative, mutual) are the foundation of healthy connection. The Adlerian alternative to praise and punishment is encouragement — acknowledging effort and contribution without creating hierarchical dependency.

## Key Vocabulary (Adlerian Terms)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Lifestyle | Self-chosen personality + worldview |
| Life tasks | Work, friendship, love — the three arenas of adult life |
| Life-lie (seikatsu-uso) | Manufactured excuses to avoid life tasks |
| Gemeinschaftsgefuhl | Community feeling / social interest |
| Minderwertigkeitsgefuhl | Feeling of inferiority (universal, healthy) |
| Inferiority complex | Using inferiority as an excuse (pathological) |
| Superiority complex | Overcompensating for inferiority via arrogance |
| Separation of tasks | Whose responsibility is this? |
| Encouragement | Horizontal support for effort (vs. praise = vertical reward) |

## Contrast with Freud

| Dimension | Freud | Adler |
|---|---|---|
| Cause of behavior | Past trauma, libido | Present goals, social goals |
| Time orientation | Past | Future |
| Unit of analysis | Individual psyche | Individual in social context |
| Core drive | Sexual/aggressive instincts | Striving for superiority (overcome inferiority) |
| Change possible? | Difficult (unconscious) | Yes, at any moment (lifestyle choice) |
| Therapy goal | Uncover repressed material | Realign lifestyle toward social interest |

## Connections

[[Alfred Adler]] developed these ideas after breaking from Freud's circle in 1911. [[Ichiro Kishimi]] and [[Fumitake Koga]] popularized them in [[courage-to-be-disliked]]. The ideas prefigure much of modern CBT, positive psychology, and ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy).
