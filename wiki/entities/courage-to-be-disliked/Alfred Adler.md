# Alfred Adler

Austrian psychiatrist (1870-1937). One of the three founders of depth psychology alongside Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung. Adler is the "unknown third giant" — less famous in popular culture than Freud or Jung despite his profound influence on modern psychology, self-help, education, and therapy.

## Core Contributions

- **Individual Psychology**: Adler's school of thought, so named because it treats the person as an indivisible whole (Latin: *individuum*) rather than as a battlefield of competing drives. Also called "Individual Psychology" to contrast with the reductive mechanics of Freudian drive theory.
- **Teleology over etiology**: Human behavior is explained by future goals (what a person is trying to achieve) rather than past causes (what happened to them). This is the foundational break from Freud.
- **Inferiority feelings**: Adler argued that all humans begin life in a position of inferiority (small, dependent) and that the drive to overcome this is the core human motivation. Universalized and de-pathologized what Freud treated as individual neurosis.
- **Gemeinschaftsgefuhl** (community feeling / social interest): The healthy endpoint of psychological development is a felt sense of belonging to and contributing to a larger community. Mental health = social embeddedness.
- **Life tasks**: Adler organized adult challenges into three domains: work, friendship, and love. Problems in living are always problems in one of these three.

## Relationship with Freud

Adler was briefly a member of Freud's Vienna Psychoanalytic Society but broke away in 1911 over irreconcilable differences: Adler rejected the libido theory, the primacy of childhood sexual trauma, and the deterministic view of the past. Freud was reportedly bitter about the separation.

## Legacy

[[Ichiro Kishimi]] translates and lectures on Adler in Japan. The book [[courage-to-be-disliked]] brought Adlerian ideas to a mass audience in East Asia. Adler's ideas prefigure much of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), positive psychology, and humanistic psychology.
